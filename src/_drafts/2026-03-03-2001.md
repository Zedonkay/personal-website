This is in no way seeking to be an objective analysis of the film. I believe that to be a far less interesting endeavour. I am seeking to explore my own personal reactions to the film, and to share them with you. I believe that Kubrick's film is a masterpiece and partially because it allows for a great deal of interpretation. This is my interpretation. An interpretation that is likely to be far more subjective than objective and one that I've been told is perhaps overly charitable but one that has become increasingly meaningful to me over time.

With that said, I would like to present the film as a triptych of the absurdity of humanity with its ending serving as a warning that evolution may be a suicide for the soul.

### I. The Kinetic Mask: Violence as Ancestry

The "Dawn of Man" isn't a miracle of the mind; it's an expansion of the fist. The bone thrown into the air is the first tool, yet its only utility is destruction. This is the first what I'll call "mask" to be presented in this film. It is an object which serves no purpose other than to conceal our primal instincts within a nice and tidy box.

This is furthered by the match-cut to the sattelite. I believe there is one interpretation of this that suggests how much we've grown, however I think another more interesting one suggesting how little we've changed. I appreciate the inrepretation that this match cut serves to show we haven't evolved past our base instincts we've just made those boxes more aerodynamic and pretty.

### II. The Semantic Mask: HAL and the Polite Apocalypse

In the "Age of Discovery" we encounter humanities use of bureaucracy as another so called mask. Dr. Heywood Floyd travels through the awe-inspiring vacuum of space, yet he never marvels at the stars. Instead, he is preoccupied with sandwiches, security clearances, and Hilton hotels. This is a "Polite Apocalypse" where we use sterile information to suppress the terror of the infinite. We have "evolved" to such a degree that we have lost our emotional vocabulary; we treat speaking to our children as an idle, administrative task.

The tragedy of this segment is HAL 9000. By forcing a machine built for total truth to maintain a human lie (the secret of the Monolith) we break him. This begins hinting at the absurdity of human existence. We have created all of these systems to hide our incredibly base level instincts and act shocked when these systems reveal our instincts themselves.

The horror of HAL's death isn't in the flickering lights but rather in the sensory contrast. As Dave Bowman methodically unscrews HAL's brain, he does so with the same cold, administrative detachment Floyd used to order a space-meal. HAL's regression into "Daisy Bell" is the only honest moment in the mission: a mechanistic system finally collapsing under the weight of human absurdity.

### III. The 4D Prison: The Rules of the Zoo

The suite at the end of "Stargate" is exactly what it claims to be. Upon seeing it I thought of Slaughterhouse-V where Billy Pilgrim is captured by Tralfamadorians and held captive in a human zoo. The tralfamadorians don't see time as distinct to the spatial dimensions and to me this suite seems like a similar concept of serving as a recreation of human life by an outside.

I think this construction in and of itself forces ourselves to acknowledge certain absurdities of our own existence. We live as though "now" is the only thing that exists but even physics suggest that space and time are a single, frozen fabric. Dave seeing an older version of himself is him seeing life as it actually is: a long, continuous physical shape that exists all at once. By turning Dave's life stages into a series of rooms, the film shows us that our birth, our dinner, and our death are just fixed coordinates on a map.

Inside this "4D Prison," the fancy furniture highlights how ridiculous our "civilization" looks when removed from context. The suite exposes the arbitrary rules we cling to—how to sit, how to eat, how to exist "properly." When Dave knocks over the wine glass, the silence is terrifying. That shattering glass is the moment the performance fails. It reveals that beneath the spacesuit and the silk robe, Dave is still just a clumsy monkey. We perform these rituals not because they matter, but because they are the only things keeping the devastating realization of bay that no matter how evolved we think we are we are still prey to the same deep seated instincts that the monkeys at the start of the film are.

### Conclusion: The Cost of Perfection

The Star Child could be seen as the "ultimate" version of humanity but I believe in many ways it is a truly pathetic creature. To become "perfect," Dave had to lose everything that made him a person. He lost his mistakes, his aging body, and his ability to touch another human being.

There is a meta-commentary here: The Monolith itself shares the exact aspect ratio of the cinema screen. When the characters stare at it, they are staring at us—the higher intelligence watching them from another dimension. We are the ones standing on the other side of the glass, watching the zoo animal eat his dinner.

Meaning requires friction. I don't love _2001_ because it shows me where we're going; I love it because it makes me realize that our "messiness" is the only thing that makes the vacuum of space bearable. If we lose that we're no better than Dr. Heywood emotionlessly communicating to his daughter from the vacuum of space.
